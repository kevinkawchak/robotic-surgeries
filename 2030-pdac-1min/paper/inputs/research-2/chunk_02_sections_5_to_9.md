## 5. Patient success rates and survival after Whipple for pancreatic cancer

Only a minority of patients with pancreatic cancer present with disease that is eligible for curative-intent resection, and the high systemic relapse rate means that survival depends on both local clearance and chemotherapy sensitivity [5,6,8].

Resection alone historically produced poor long-term survival, and modern outcomes improved after randomized trials established effective adjuvant therapy [7,8,9].

CONKO-001 showed that adjuvant gemcitabine after macroscopically complete resection improved disease-free survival and overall survival compared with observation, with five-year overall survival of 20.7 percent versus 10.4 percent and ten-year overall survival of 12.2 percent versus 7.7 percent [9].

ESPAC-4 showed that gemcitabine-capecitabine improved survival over gemcitabine alone, and the 2025 long-term analysis reported median overall survival of 31.6 months with gemcitabine-capecitabine versus 28.4 months with gemcitabine alone in the whole cohort [7].

ESPAC-4 long-term data showed a particularly large exploratory benefit in R0-resected patients, with median survival of 49.9 months for gemcitabine-capecitabine versus 32.2 months for gemcitabine alone [7].

PRODIGE 24/CCTG PA.6 remains the strongest adjuvant survival benchmark for fit patients, with median overall survival of 53.5 months for modified FOLFIRINOX versus 35.5 months for gemcitabine and five-year overall survival of 43.2 percent versus 31.4 percent [8].

APACT did not displace modified FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine-capecitabine as the preferred adjuvant standards, but it contributed evidence on gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel after resection and highlighted the difficulty of improving outcomes in all-comer resected PDAC [10].

A realistic contemporary patient-success benchmark after Whipple for PDAC is not simply hospital discharge; it is survival without major complication, R0 resection when technically feasible, postoperative recovery sufficient for systemic therapy, no early recurrence, and preservation of pancreatic exocrine, endocrine, nutritional, and functional status [5,6,7,8].

For AI-assisted robotic trials, recurrence-free survival, overall survival, time to adjuvant therapy, patient-reported recovery, and quality-of-life measures should be included because a shorter incision or hospital stay has limited value if oncologic therapy is delayed or margin quality is compromised [5,6,41,43].

## 6. Death rates and preventable mortality pathways

Historical Whipple mortality was high enough that some mid-20th-century and early-late-20th-century debates questioned whether resection was justified for pancreatic adenocarcinoma when operative mortality approached or exceeded long-term survival probability [1,2].

Modern high-volume centers commonly report perioperative mortality below 5 percent, but population-level outcomes remain sensitive to hospital volume, surgeon volume, patient frailty, emergency rescue capacity, and access to interventional radiology and intensive care [2,3,4,20].

The volume-outcome literature remains one of the most reproducible findings in pancreatic surgery, and a 2023 systematic review concluded that hospital and surgeon volume indicators are associated with postoperative outcomes while emphasizing the need for valid outcome assessment [4].

The leading preventable death pathway after pancreaticoduodenectomy is frequently a complication cascade rather than a single intraoperative catastrophe, with postoperative pancreatic fistula leading to abscess, sepsis, pseudoaneurysm, postpancreatectomy hemorrhage, organ failure, and death when rescue fails [17,18,20,21].

The ISGPS 2026 mortality framework matters because it shifts quality review from crude death counts to root-cause-linked mortality categories, enabling better learning from failure-to-rescue events [20].

AI-assisted robotic systems should therefore be evaluated against death-mechanism endpoints such as unrecognized vascular injury, delayed recognition of pancreatic leak, failure to detect early hemorrhage, inadequate drain or imaging algorithms, and postoperative deterioration prediction [20,46,47,48].

## 7. Morbidity: pancreatic fistula, hemorrhage, delayed gastric emptying, and failure to rescue

The 2016 ISGPS update, published in 2017, replaced the former grade A pancreatic fistula with the concept of a biochemical leak and preserved grade B and C as clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula categories [17].

Clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula remains one of the central complications after Whipple, and a 2022 meta-analysis estimated an incidence of 17 percent with risk factors including male sex, BMI above 25 kg/m2, pancreatic duct diameter below 3 mm, soft pancreatic texture, and blood transfusion [23].

The original Fistula Risk Score prospectively validated a 10-point intraoperative score using gland texture, duct size, pathology, and blood loss, with negligible-risk patients having no clinically relevant fistulas and the highest-risk scores having very high fistula rates [21].

The alternative Fistula Risk Score removed blood loss and used soft texture, duct diameter, and BMI, making it more practical for modern datasets and for preoperative and intraoperative decision support [22].

Postpancreatectomy hemorrhage is a high-mortality complication because it can reflect arterial erosion from pancreatic fistula, pseudoaneurysm, intra-abdominal sepsis, or technical bleeding, and ISGPS standardized its reporting by timing, location, and severity [18].

Delayed gastric emptying is common after pancreatic surgery, and ISGPS created an objective severity grading system because historical incidence rates varied widely across centers and definitions [19].

A comprehensive 2017 review found that the ISGPS definition became the dominant standard after 2010 and reported average delayed gastric emptying of 27.7 percent and clinically relevant delayed gastric emptying of 14.3 percent after pancreaticoduodenectomy [19].

Early drain removal in properly selected low- or intermediate-risk patients with reassuring drain amylase can reduce drain-related complications and is now supported by randomized data and systematic review evidence [24,25,29].

Pancreatic duct stenting remains a debated risk-reduction tool, especially in high-risk anastomoses, and 2025 systematic review evidence focused on whether stent placement reduces clinically relevant fistula in high-risk settings [26].

Pasireotide reduced clinically significant pancreatic fistula, leak, or abscess in a single-center randomized trial, but adoption has varied because external replication, cost, patient selection, and institutional baseline fistula rates affect the perceived value of prophylactic somatostatin analog strategies [30].

## 8. Perioperative care breakthroughs

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery pathways for pancreaticoduodenectomy standardize preoperative counseling, nutrition, analgesia, mobilization, glycemic management, nasogastric-tube avoidance when possible, early feeding, drain algorithms, and discharge criteria [27,28].

Updated ERAS evidence suggests shorter length of stay and lower costs without compromising safety when pathways are implemented with multidisciplinary adherence rather than as a checklist alone [28].

Preoperative optimization now includes biliary sepsis control, selective biliary drainage rather than routine drainage, nutrition and sarcopenia assessment, diabetes management, smoking cessation, frailty assessment, anemia and blood management, and postoperative pancreatic enzyme replacement when needed [5,6,27,28].

Routine preoperative biliary drainage is not a universal requirement for resectable pancreatic head cancer, but it is often necessary for cholangitis, severe symptomatic jaundice, renal dysfunction, delayed surgery due to neoadjuvant therapy, or when chemotherapy cannot safely begin without biliary decompression [5,6].

Modern blood management matters because transfusion is associated with infectious and fistula-related risk in observational pancreatic surgery literature, while blood-sparing surgery and restrictive transfusion policies can support recovery when clinically appropriate [23].

The strongest perioperative safety programs combine standardized definitions, prospective audit, central review, morbidity and mortality conferences, failure-to-rescue analysis, and benchmarking across institutions [4,20,44].

## 9. Open, laparoscopic, and robotic Whipple evidence

The first laparoscopic pylorus-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy was reported by Gagner and Pomp in 1994, but laparoscopic adoption was slow because the operation requires retroperitoneal dissection near major vessels and three complex reconstructions [34].

The LEOPARD-2 experience became a cautionary milestone because it demonstrated that minimally invasive pancreaticoduodenectomy could be unsafe during early implementation even when the approach seemed technically feasible [35].

PADULAP showed that laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy could shorten length of stay and improve some postoperative recovery metrics in selected patients at an expert center, but operative time was longer and generalizability remained limited [36].

The 2024 three-year follow-up of a Chinese randomized trial comparing laparoscopic and open pancreaticoduodenectomy for pancreatic and periampullary tumors added medium-term outcome data and helped shift minimally invasive Whipple evidence from purely short-term feasibility toward oncologic follow-up [37].

A 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials of minimally invasive pancreaticoduodenectomy found similar mortality and major morbidity between minimally invasive and open approaches across seven randomized studies and 1428 patients, with mortality of 2.9 percent for minimally invasive pancreaticoduodenectomy and 2.6 percent for open pancreaticoduodenectomy [38].

Robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy was first performed by Pier Cristoforo Giulianotti in 2001 and reported in 2003, and robotic systems were proposed to overcome laparoscopic limitations in suturing, visualization, ergonomics, and wristed reconstruction [39,40].

The Chinese multicenter randomized trial of robotic versus open pancreaticoduodenectomy reported shorter postoperative length of stay for robotic surgery, with median 11.0 days versus 13.5 days, while 90-day mortality and severe complications were similar and each group had one in-hospital death within 90 days [41].

The EUROPA randomized stage 2b trial at Heidelberg was an important European trial because it used cumulative morbidity within 90 days as the primary endpoint and applied IDEAL-stage logic to robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy evaluation [42].

A 2024 meta-analysis that included two randomized trials and 22 propensity-matched studies found that robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy had longer operative time but lower blood loss, shorter hospital stay, lower transfusion, and lower wound infection, with no significant differences in 90-day mortality, major complications, postoperative pancreatic fistula, postpancreatectomy hemorrhage, delayed gastric emptying, lymph node yield, or R0 resection [43].

The 2025 Dutch nationwide 1000-case robotic cohort showed that robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy can be implemented nationally with training, but it also showed that major morbidity and mortality did not improve across learning phases even when operative time, conversion, delayed gastric emptying, reoperation, and hospital stay improved [44].

The strongest current conclusion is that robotic Whipple is feasible and can produce comparable short-term safety in expert, high-volume settings, but it is not yet proven to improve long-term survival, eliminate fistula, or justify autonomous execution outside controlled programs [41,42,43,44,45].
