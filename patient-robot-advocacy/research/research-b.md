Main Patient Concerns in Surgical-Robot and Physical-AI Oncology Trials

Scope

In this document, Physical AI means an AI-enabled system that uses sensor, imaging, instrument, or patient data to recommend, plan, or carry out a physical action during an intervention. This may range from conventional robot-assisted surgery, in which the surgeon continuously controls the instruments, to systems with task-level or conditional autonomy.

The distinction matters. Most established robot-assisted surgical systems remain under direct surgeon control, whereas newer systems may independently generate plans or execute defined tasks under supervision [5,6]. Patients therefore need a precise explanation of what the machine does, what the surgeon does, and who can intervene at every stage.  ￼

Patients are not necessarily opposed to these technologies. A 2026 qualitative study found that 56% of 50 previously operated patients would consider semi-autonomous robot-assisted surgery, but willingness depended strongly on which parts of the procedure the robot controlled. The principal themes were informed consent, additional risks, surgeon–patient trust, and anticipated benefits [1]. In a separate survey of 330 oncology patients, approximately half expressed concern about AI in cancer care, particularly loss of human interaction and medical errors [2].  ￼

Main concerns

Concern	What patients are likely to ask	What the trial team should explain
1. Safety, malfunction, and unintended actions	“Could the system move unexpectedly, misidentify tissue, lose visualization, freeze, drift, or damage a nearby organ?”	Describe known and anticipated failure modes, preclinical testing, safety limits, independent monitoring, emergency-stop functions, backup equipment, and criteria for converting to conventional laparoscopic or open surgery [1,3,4,6].  ￼
2. Who is actually controlling the operation?	“Is my surgeon controlling every movement, approving a plan, supervising an automated task, or allowing the system to make decisions?”	State the system’s autonomy level for each procedural step. Avoid simply calling the system “robotic” or “AI-assisted,” because those terms do not reveal whether it only provides information, moves under direct control, or performs an action independently [1,3,5,6].  ￼
3. Human override and rescue capability	“Can the surgeon stop the AI immediately? How quickly can human control be restored?”	Explain who watches the system, what triggers intervention, whether the surgeon can override every action, how long disengagement takes, and what the fallback workflow is if the system becomes unreliable [1,5,7].  ￼
4. Responsibility if something goes wrong	“Who is accountable—the surgeon, hospital, manufacturer, software developer, sponsor, or AI itself?”	Identify the responsible clinical decision-maker and explain how adverse events, device malfunctions, algorithmic errors, protocol deviations, and research-related injuries will be investigated. Increasing autonomy can make traditional divisions of responsibility less clear [1,4,5].  ￼
5. Surgeon and operating-team experience	“How many procedures has my surgeon performed using this exact robot, software version, and level of autonomy?”	Report experience with the specific platform and procedure—not only general surgical experience. Explain credentialing, simulation training, proctoring, team training, learning-curve monitoring, and whether trainees will participate [1,3,6].  ￼
6. Cancer-control effectiveness	“Will this remove my cancer as completely as standard surgery?”	Prioritize oncologic outcomes such as margin status, tumor disruption, lymph-node assessment, local recurrence, disease-free survival, overall survival, and delays to chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Smaller incisions or shorter hospitalization should not be presented as evidence of equivalent cancer control. FDA information notes that cancer-related long-term outcomes have not been established for every use of robot-assisted surgical devices [6].  ￼
7. Unknown and experimental risks	“Am I one of the first people to receive this procedure? What is still unknown?”	State whether the trial primarily evaluates feasibility, technical performance, safety, clinical effectiveness, or superiority. Explain the number of prior human procedures, follow-up duration, unresolved risks, stopping rules, and what evidence would cause the study to pause [11,13].  ￼
8. Randomization and treatment choice	“Could I be assigned to a treatment I would not have chosen?”	Explain the study arms, allocation probability, standard-care comparator, masking if applicable, crossover rules, and whether the patient or surgeon can choose the assigned approach. In a randomized study, neither the patient nor physician normally chooses the group [11,13].  ￼
9. Overreliance on AI or automation bias	“Will the surgeon follow the AI even when their clinical judgment suggests something different?”	Explain that the performance of the human–AI team is being evaluated. Describe how clinicians are trained to question outputs, recognize uncertainty, disregard unsafe recommendations, document overrides, and prevent complacency or automation bias [5,7].  ￼
10. Privacy, recording, and secondary data use	“Will my operation be recorded? Who will see the video and data, and will they be used to train other AI systems?”	Identify every data type collected, including video, audio, imaging, instrument motion, device logs, anatomy, pathology, outcomes, and clinician actions. Explain de-identification, retention, access, international transfer, commercial use, future model training, withdrawal options, and whether data may be shared with manufacturers [1,2,7,11].  ￼
11. Cybersecurity and network dependence	“Could someone hack the system or interfere with the operation?”	Explain whether the device is connected to hospital networks, external servers, cloud services, or remote-support personnel. Describe authentication, encryption, software-update controls, cybersecurity testing, incident response, offline operation, and what happens if connectivity is interrupted [8].  ￼
12. Bias and applicability to the individual patient	“Was the system tested on people with my anatomy, age, sex, race, body size, disability, previous treatments, and tumor type?”	Provide the demographic and clinical composition of training, validation, and trial populations. Report subgroup performance and important exclusions, particularly where anatomy, prior radiation, obesity, comorbidities, tumor location, or demographic factors may affect performance [2,9,10].  ￼
13. Software changes, versioning, and performance drift	“Could the software change after I consent? Are all participants receiving the same system?”	Identify the exact hardware and software versions used. Explain whether updates are allowed during the trial, how they are validated, whether participants will be re-consented, and how investigators will detect performance degradation caused by changing patients, workflows, equipment, or clinical practices [7,10].  ￼
14. Loss of personal care and surgeon–patient trust	“Will I still have a surgeon who knows me and is personally responsible for my care?”	Reassure patients that technology does not replace communication, empathy, individualized judgment, postoperative care, or accountability. Patient willingness is influenced strongly by trust in the surgeon and preservation of the clinician–patient relationship [1–3].  ￼
15. Marketing, hype, and unrealistic expectations	“Is this actually better for my cancer, or is it simply newer and described as more precise?”	Use neutral language and present absolute evidence rather than terms such as “revolutionary,” “state of the art,” or “cutting-edge.” Patients should understand that technical precision, smaller incisions, or a newer platform do not automatically produce better oncologic outcomes [3,4,6].  ￼
16. Practical, financial, and post-trial burdens	“Will I have extra visits, travel, scans, costs, or follow-up? Who pays if I am injured?”	Explain additional procedures, questionnaires, imaging, long-term surveillance, travel, lodging, missed work, caregiving needs, insurance coverage, sponsor-paid costs, research-related injury arrangements, and what care or device support remains available after the trial ends [11,12].  ￼

Minimum information patients should receive before enrollment

A strong consent and shared-decision process should clearly address the following:
	1.	The exact role of the robot and AI.
Identify every procedural step in which the system observes, recommends, plans, restricts movement, moves under direct control, or acts semi-autonomously [1,5–7].
	2.	The division of control between the system and the clinical team.
State who initiates each action, who approves it, who supervises it, and who may interrupt or reverse it [1,5].
	3.	The evidence supporting the procedure.
Separate technical or short-term benefits from established oncologic outcomes. Present relevant conventional, laparoscopic, and robot-assisted alternatives [3,6,11].
	4.	Known, anticipated, and unknown risks.
Include risks created by hardware, software, AI recommendations, autonomous actions, data quality, human–machine interaction, and the underlying surgical procedure [1,5,10,11].
	5.	The failure and conversion plan.
Describe emergency stopping, system disengagement, backup equipment, loss of power or connectivity, and criteria for converting to another surgical method [1,6,8].
	6.	The experience of the surgeon and operating team.
Disclose training and experience with the exact device, software version, autonomy level, cancer procedure, and institutional workflow [1,3,6].
	7.	Data governance and cybersecurity.
Explain what is collected, who receives it, how long it is retained, whether it may be reused commercially or for future AI training, and how unauthorized access will be prevented [1,7,8,11].
	8.	Population representativeness.
Explain how closely the development and trial populations resemble the individual patient and disclose known subgroup limitations [2,9,10].
	9.	Software lifecycle management.
State whether the system can be updated during the trial, how version changes are controlled, how model drift is monitored, and when new information will require re-consent [7,10].
	10.	Trial mechanics and alternatives.
Explain randomization, control groups, additional procedures, voluntary participation, withdrawal rights, standard-treatment alternatives, follow-up requirements, costs, and research-related injury provisions [11–13].

Bottom line

The central patient concern is unlikely to be simply, “Do I trust robots?” It is more likely to be:

“Am I accepting additional uncertainty or giving up human judgment, privacy, accountability, or cancer-control effectiveness for a technology whose benefits have not yet been proved for someone like me?”

Trust will depend less on promotional claims about technological sophistication and more on demonstrable safety, transparent allocation of control, experienced clinicians, meaningful cancer outcomes, strong data protections, fair participant selection, and an immediate human rescue pathway [1–10].
